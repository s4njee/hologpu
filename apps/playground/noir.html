The Mercer Standard

THE LATE EDITION · NOVEMBER 17, 1955

The rain came down.
The truth went under.

A missing girl. A dead man’s address. And the last edition of a newspaper that knew too much.

BY ELIAS VALE, STAFF CORRESPONDENTFiled at 1:47 a.m.

The rain had been falling for six days. By the seventh, it had learned the names of everyone who owed the city something. It tapped them out on the windows of the night desk, one letter at a time.

I was writing up a warehouse fire nobody had seen when the girl came in. No umbrella. No coat. Just a wet envelope held flat against her ribs, as if it were the only thing keeping her together.

“You print the names of the dead,” she said. “I need you to stop printing one of the living.”

Her brother’s obituary had appeared in Tuesday’s edition. Thirty-two years old. Dockworker. Survived by no one. She had spoken to him on Wednesday.

I checked the carbon. There was my byline, crooked as a bad tooth. There was my signature at the bottom. I didn’t remember writing a word of it.

“Where is he now?” I asked.

“That’s what I came to find out.”

The address in the envelope was a boarding house on Bellweather. I knew the place. Everyone did. The police passed it slowly and looked straight ahead. The landlady collected rent in cash and never asked why a man needed a second name.

Room nine had been emptied. Not cleaned. Emptied. A pale rectangle marked where a bed had stood. The wardrobe doors hung open. On the sill, a glass of water was still trembling from the trains beneath the street.

In this town, a man could disappear twice. Once from the street. Once from the record.

Under the loose floorboard I found a ledger. Twenty-three names. Every one of them had an obituary in the Standard. Every one of them had drawn a city paycheck the following week.

The girl watched me turn the pages. “He said they were moving something through the docks. He said the papers would listen.”

Outside, a sedan idled without headlights. Its windshield wipers kept time with a song nobody wanted to hear.

I told her to take the stairs at the back. She asked whether I was coming. I said I had a deadline. It sounded braver than it was.

By the time I reached the office, the night editor’s lamp was out. His coffee was warm. A fresh ribbon sat beside my typewriter. Somebody had taken the trouble to oil the keys.

I called the precinct. The desk sergeant listened without interrupting. Then he asked where I was calling from, although I hadn’t told him my name.

I put down the receiver.

The story came out of the machine in hard little blows. The ledger. The docks. The men who were dead on paper and working in the dark. I made three carbons. Old habit. A story only needs one copy to live; sometimes it needs three to survive.

At two-thirteen, the presses downstairs stopped.

I pulled the page from the roller. There was a knock at the door. Polite. Patient. The kind of knock that already knows the lock is useless.

Then I saw the sheet waiting in my out-tray.

My obituary. Forty-one years old. Staff correspondent. Survived by no one.

The date was tomorrow’s.

I fed another page into the machine. Outside, the rain kept tapping its names against the glass. This time, I tapped back.

At dawn, one copy reached the street.
